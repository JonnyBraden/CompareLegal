Solicitors charge for estate work in three ways: a fixed fee, an hourly rate, or a percentage of the estate. Sometimes they combine them, for example an hourly rate plus a percentage. There is no official scale, and no typical percentage set by a court or a regulator. The useful comparison is the same job, priced in pounds, with VAT and disbursements included. You can [compare probate fees](/probate/) on that basis. The fees and rules below were checked on 21 November 2026.

[Probate costs across the UK](/blog/probate-costs-uk/) lists the court fees. In Scotland the court issues confirmation, and [confirmation costs in Scotland](/blog/confirmation-costs-scotland/) covers that fee on its own.

## Two different jobs

Firms usually offer two levels of service, and a price only makes sense once you know which one you are buying.

- **Grant only, or confirmation only.** The firm prepares the application and the tax forms and obtains the grant or confirmation. You then collect the assets and pay the beneficiaries. This is often a fixed fee.
- **Full administration, called executry in Scotland.** The firm values the assets, deals with banks and pension providers, arranges the sale or transfer of property, pays debts and tax, and distributes the estate. This is where a percentage, an hourly rate or a larger fixed fee usually appears.

A low fixed fee for the grant is a different service from a percentage for the whole estate. Compare them only after each quote names the work.

## How a percentage fee behaves

A percentage moves with the value of the estate. The same percentage applied to a house produces a much larger fee than the same percentage applied to a small current account, even when the forms are much the same. That is the arithmetic. It does not tell you whether a percentage will come out above or below a fixed fee for your estate. On a small estate the percentage can be the lower figure. On a large estate made up of a house and a few accounts, it can be far higher than the work involved.

Before you accept a percentage, ask the firm to convert it into pounds for your estate, and ask:

- Which figure is the percentage applied to: the gross estate, the estate after debts, or only the assets the firm collects?
- Does the home count? Does a house that passes automatically to a joint owner count?
- Is there a minimum fee?
- Is an hourly rate added for part of the work?
- What sits outside the percentage: selling a property, a dispute, an inheritance tax enquiry, a business, or assets abroad?
- Is VAT added on top?

VAT at 20% is charged on the legal fee when the firm is registered. The court fee is a disbursement. It is a set amount, and it is not worked out as a percentage of the estate. In England and Wales it is £526 when the estate is worth more than £5,000, from 13 July 2026, and there is no fee at £5,000 or below. In Northern Ireland, from 1 April 2026, the grant fee is £326 when the assessed value is over £10,000, and nothing at £10,000 or below. Someone applying without a solicitor pays a further £81 on an estate over £10,000.

## How a fixed fee behaves

A fixed fee tells you the legal fee at the outset, for a piece of work the firm has described. The questions to ask are what sits inside it, and what would move the matter off it. A leasehold flat to sell, a missing beneficiary, a claim against the estate, or papers that do not match the will are the usual reasons a fixed fee is revised. Ask for those reasons in writing, in the client care letter. [Questions to ask a solicitor](/blog/questions-to-ask-before-instructing-a-solicitor/) puts that in a short list.

## What firms must publish

In England and Wales, the Solicitors Regulation Authority's Transparency Rules require firms that offer probate to publish prices for uncontested cases where all the assets are in the UK. The published price has to say what is included, what a client might reasonably expect to be included and is not, and whether VAT is included. If the firm shows a range, it has to explain the basis, including any hourly rate and the factors that decide where in the range a matter will fall. Typical timescales and the main stages have to be there too.

Read that page, then ask the firm to turn it into a figure for your estate. The published range is the starting point. It is not yet a quote.

Scotland and Northern Ireland do not have that same publication rule. Solicitors there are regulated by the Law Society of Scotland and the Law Society of Northern Ireland. Ask for the same breakdown in writing: the job, the fee in pounds, VAT, and the disbursements. The confirmation fee, or the Northern Ireland probate fee, should be named rather than folded into a percentage you cannot see.

## A way to choose

1. Write down the job. Grant or confirmation only, or full administration.
2. List the assets. Note anything abroad, a business, a trust, or a likely disagreement.
3. Ask two or three firms for a price in pounds for that job, including VAT and the disbursements they expect.
4. If a firm quotes a percentage, ask for that percentage worked out on your estate, and ask whether they will also quote a fixed fee for the same work.
5. Compare the totals for the same scope. A grant-only quote and a full-administration quote are answering different questions.
6. Ask who will do the work, and how long they expect it to take.

A fixed fee is the clearer figure when the firm can see the work: there is a will, the assets are in the UK, and nothing is in dispute. An hourly rate, or a percentage with a ceiling, fits better when the firm cannot yet see the work. Missing papers, a business, a claim against the estate, or assets in another country are that kind of case. Ask for the ceiling, or for the work to be done in stages, so the bill cannot rise without you agreeing the next stage.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there a standard percentage for probate?

No. Each firm sets its own fixed fee, hourly rate or percentage. Courts and regulators do not publish a rate that firms must use. Ask for the price of your estate in pounds.

### Do solicitors have to publish probate prices?

In England and Wales, yes, for uncontested probate where all the assets are in the UK. The SRA Transparency Rules require the price, what it covers, and whether VAT is included. Scotland and Northern Ireland do not have the same rule. You can still ask for a written quote, and [compare firms that list their fees](/probate/).

### Does the percentage include the court fee?

Treat the court fee as extra unless the quote says it is included. In England and Wales the probate application is £526 if the estate is over £5,000. In Scotland the confirmation fee depends on the value of the estate. Both are set out in [probate costs across the UK](/blog/probate-costs-uk/).

### Which is the better choice for a straightforward estate?

Where there is a will, the assets are in the UK, and nothing is disputed, a fixed fee for a clearly described job is the easier figure to compare. Ask what would take the estate outside that fee before you instruct the firm.
