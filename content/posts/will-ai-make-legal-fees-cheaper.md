Will AI make legal fees cheaper? For some kinds of legal work, it may start to. Firms that handle transactional work such as commercial contracts, leases and share purchases are adopting AI drafting and review tools quickly, and that could change how those deals are priced over time. High-street work is different. Conveyancing, wills, probate and family law have far fewer major AI tools built specifically for them, so if you are buying a home, making a will or dealing with an estate, prices may not come down as quickly as the headlines suggest. This guide explains why, and what to ask a firm before you instruct them.

## How transactional law firms are using AI

Firms that do corporate and commercial work, such as share purchases, investment rounds, commercial leases and supply agreements, have been among the first to adopt AI. That work involves long documents built from a firm's own precedents, rounds of negotiation and detailed review, which is where drafting and review tools save the most time.

The tools vary. Some firms use general workplace software such as Microsoft Copilot. Others use legal platforms such as Harvey or Legora, or Word add-ins such as Spellbook and [Scriptum AI](https://tryscriptum.ai). Scriptum AI is a Microsoft Word add-in that drafts from a firm's own precedents and is built for UK and Irish transactional firms with between 5 and 500 fee earners. None of these tools replaces the solicitor. The firm remains responsible for the advice and the documents you receive.

The [Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA)](https://www.sra.org.uk/sra/research-publications/artificial-intelligence-legal-market/) has said that automating parts of a matter can reduce the time spent on it, and therefore its cost, which could benefit both firms and clients. As these tools become routine on transactional work, firms competing for that work may come under pressure to reflect the time saved in their quotes.

## Why high-street fees may not fall as quickly

Most of the investment in legal AI so far has gone into tools for corporate, commercial and litigation work at larger firms. High-street practice areas have far fewer major tools built specifically for them.

- **Conveyancing.** There are AI tools for residential conveyancers, mostly for reading title documents and search results and flagging issues. They speed up parts of the file, but much of a purchase is waiting on lenders, searches, other firms in the chain and HM Land Registry, which software cannot shorten.
- **Wills and powers of attorney.** Will drafting already relies heavily on standard templates and case management software. The time-consuming part is taking instructions, checking capacity and making sure the will is signed and witnessed correctly.
- **Probate and estates.** Much of the work is gathering information from banks, pension providers and HMRC, and dealing with the probate registry. AI can help with letters and summaries, but it does not make third parties reply faster.
- **Family law.** Divorce and financial matters depend on advice, negotiation and court timetables, and are often emotionally difficult. There are few tools built for this work.

There is also less room for prices to fall. High-street work is already often sold on fixed fees with tight margins, and for some services, such as uncontested probate and residential conveyancing in England and Wales, firms must publish their prices. On a conveyancing quote, much of what you pay is tax and disbursements that have nothing to do with how the firm works.

AI may still make high-street firms faster or more consistent, and over time it could feed into lower prices. But for now, the most reliable way to pay less for high-street work is to compare quotes, not to wait for AI to bring fees down.

## What the rules say about AI and pricing

The [SRA's rules](https://www.sra.org.uk/solicitors/standards-regulations/code-conduct-solicitors/) in England and Wales require solicitors to give clients the best possible information about how a matter will be priced and its likely overall cost. The SRA has also [advised firms](https://www.sra.org.uk/sra/research-publications/artificial-intelligence-legal-market/) to tell clients when they will be using AI on a case and how it will operate. The [Law Society of England and Wales](https://www.lawsociety.org.uk/topics/ai-and-lawtech/generative-ai-the-essentials) similarly encourages firms to discuss and clearly communicate their use of generative AI with clients.

Those duties do not, by themselves, require a firm to cut its fees. If a firm uses AI, it may finish the work in fewer hours, offer a more competitive fixed fee, or keep the same price and improve its own margins. If you want any efficiency saving reflected in the price, you need to ask, compare quotes, and get the answer in the client care letter.

Solicitors in Scotland and Northern Ireland are regulated separately, by the Law Society of Scotland and the Law Society of Northern Ireland. You can still ask the same practical questions about tools, review and price.

## Fixed fees and hourly billing

How the firm charges matters as much as whether it uses AI.

**Hourly rates.** These are more common on commercial and transactional work. If you are billed for time spent, fewer hours should usually mean a lower bill, provided the firm records time honestly and does not raise its rate to cancel out the saving. Ask whether the estimate already assumes the use of AI.

**Fixed fees.** These are the norm for conveyancing, wills, uncontested probate and uncontested divorce. A fixed fee can be good value if the scope is clear, because you know the legal fee in advance. It also means the firm, not you, keeps the benefit of any time saved unless the quoted fee comes down. Ask what is included, what would make the fee go up, and whether the quote reflects the firm's use of AI.

**Capped fees.** A cap limits what you pay for the agreed work. Check what sits outside the cap, and whether AI use is already built into the estimate beneath it.

VAT and disbursements sit on top of the legal fee. Compare the total, not just the headline figure.

## Questions to ask your solicitor about AI and fees

Get the answers in writing before you instruct the firm.

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Do you use AI on this kind of work, and which tools? | So you know how the matter will be handled, not just the price. |
| Who checks the output, and who is responsible if something is wrong? | The solicitor remains accountable for the work, including anything a tool helped to produce. |
| Does this fee already reflect any time saved by AI? | Efficiency savings are not passed on automatically. |
| If you charge by the hour, will I be billed only for time actually spent? | You should not pay for hours the software removed from the job. |
| If this is a fixed or capped fee, what is included and what would make it go up? | The scope matters as much as the number. |
| Will any of my documents or confidential information be put into an AI tool? | Firms must protect client confidentiality. Ask where the data goes and who can see it. |
| Can I see this in the client care letter? | If it is not written down, it is harder to rely on later. |

## Frequently asked questions

### Will AI make conveyancing cheaper?

Not quickly. Some conveyancers use AI to read title documents and searches, but much of a purchase is waiting on lenders, searches and other firms in the chain, and much of the total is tax and disbursements. Comparing quotes is still the best way to pay less.

### Does a firm have to tell me if it uses AI?

In England and Wales, the SRA has advised firms to tell clients when they will be using AI on their case, and how it will operate. The Law Society also recommends clear communication. Ask directly, and get the answer in the client care letter.

### If a firm uses AI, should its quote be lower?

Not necessarily. AI may make a firm faster or more consistent without changing the fee. Compare the total price, what is included, who will review the work, and how long it is likely to take.

### Does AI change stamp duty, searches or court fees?

No. Those are payments to other organisations, often called disbursements. They are the same whoever you instruct and whether or not the firm uses AI.
